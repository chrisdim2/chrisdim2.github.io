The short answer: in Greek, chronos simply means time. The story behind the word is more interesting, though, and it hides a second word that the ancient Greeks kept carefully apart from the first.

## Chronos in Greek myth

In Greek mythology Chronos is the personification of time itself. In antiquity he was often identified with Cronus, the Titan who ruled in the Golden Age before Zeus. That identification later gave us "Father Time" with his scythe, a scythe that first belonged to Cronus.

The two names sound almost the same, but the word chronos is not thought to be related to the name Cronus, whose origin is still uncertain. Either way, the ancients did not let the likeness go to waste.

## Chronos and Kairos: two words for time

The ancient Greeks told apart two kinds of time:

- **Chronos** is the time you can count: minutes, days, years, one after the other.
- **Kairos** is the right moment, the one you either catch or miss.

Kairos had a figure of his own. The sculptor Lysippos set up a bronze statue of him in Sicyon: a young boy with long hair in front and a bare back of the head. The poet Posidippus, in an epigram on the statue, explains why: you grab Kairos by the hair as he comes towards you; once he has passed, there is nothing left to hold on to. The bronze original is lost; descriptions and later copies survive.

And one more thing every Greek knows: in modern Greek, kairos also means the weather.

## Why it suits a calendar

A calendar keeps your time: everything you need to remember, in the order it comes. [Chronos](/en/chronos/) also shows the weather next to your plans, hour by hour, for every day and for every event with a place. So you can pick the right moment too. Chronos and kairos, on the same screen.

The name also passed to the family of apps that grew out of Chronos, such as [Chronos Name Days](/en/giortes/).
